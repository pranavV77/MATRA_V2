1 Structural and electronic properties of Ga 2O3-Al2O3 alloys
2AQ1 Hartwin Peelaers,1 Joel B. Varley,2 James S. Speck,1 and Chris G. Van de Walle1
3 1Materials Department, University of California, Santa Barbara, California 93106-5050, USA
4 2Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Livermore, California 94550, USA
5 (Received 20 April 2018; accepted 27 May 2018; published online xx xx xxxx)
6 Ga2O3 is emerging as an important electronic material. Alloying with Al 2O3 is a viable method to
7 achieve carrier conﬁnement, to increase the bandgap, or to modify the lattice parameters. However,
8 the two materials have very different ground-state crystal structures (monoclinic b-gallia for Ga2O3
9 and corundum for Al 2O3). Here, we use hybrid density functional theory calculations to assess the
10 alloy stabilities and electronic properties of the alloys. We ﬁnd that the monoclinic phase is the pre-
11 ferred structure for up to 71% Al incorporation, in close agreement with experimental phase dia-
12 grams, and that the ordered monoclinic AlGaO 3 alloy is exceptionally stable. We also discuss
13 bandgap bowing, lattice constants, and band offsets that can guide future synthesis and device
14 design efforts. Published by AIP Publishing. https://doi.org/10.1063/1.5036991
15 Ga2O3 is a wide-bandgap semiconductor with a funda-
16 mental bandgap of 4.76 eV. 1 Its transparency into the UV
17 allows applications such as solar-blind photodetectors 2 and
18 deep-UV transparent contacts. 3 Its high breakdown voltage
19 and low effective mass and the ability to achieve controllable
20 n-type doping 4–6 render it suitable for high-voltage ﬁeld
21 effect transistors 7,8 and Schottky barrier diodes. 9 p-Type
22 doping is unlikely to occur.10,11 These properties, along with
23 the availability of high-quality single-crystal substrates, ren-
24 der Ga2O3 a promising material for next-generation devices.
25 Many applications require a material with a bandgap
26 even larger than Ga2O3, as a dielectric, or to form heterostruc-
27 tures for carrier conﬁnement, or to enable optoelectronics
28 deeper into the UV.12 Al2O3 is an excellent candidate, with a
29 bandgap of 8.82 eV in the corundum (sapphire) phase. 13
30 Sapphire can also be used as a substrate for the growth of
31 Ga2O3.14–18 Alloy formation also permits tuning of the lattice
32 parameters, which can minimize stress when the material is
33 grown on a substrate and may offer additional control over
34 the transport properties. Ga2O3-Al2O3 alloys [(AlxGa1/C0 x)2O3]
35 have already been grown using chemical vapor deposition
36 (CVD),14–16 pulsed laser deposition (PLD),17–20 or molecular
37 beam epitaxy (MBE)12,21–23 or through grinding or solid com-
38 bustion synthesis.24–28
39 The ground-state crystal structures of Ga 2O3 and Al2O3
40 are very different: Ga 2O3 adopts the monoclinic ( b) phase
41 with symmetry group C2/m, while Al 2O3 adopts the corun-
42 dum (a) phase with symmetry group R/C223c. Corundum Ga2O3
43 is also known as a-Ga2O3, while the h phase of Al 2O3 has
44 the same structure as the monoclinic b phase of Ga2O3. The
45 primitive cells of both structures contain 10 atoms (see insets
46 of Fig. 1). In the monoclinic phase, two types of cation envi-
47 ronments are present: an octahedrally coordinated and a tet-
48 rahedrally coordinated site. In contrast, only octahedrally
49 coordinated cation sites are present in the corundum phase.
50 These different crystal structures and coordination environ-
51 ments raise the question what the preferred crystal structure
52 will be in (Al xGa1/C0 x)2O3 alloys as a function of the alloy
53 composition. Experimentally reported upper limits on the
54 stability of the monoclinic structure have exhibited a wide
55range, with maximum Al concentrations ranging from 67 to
5685%.14,17–19,21,25–27 For the corundum structure, the upper
57limit is lower, with maximum Ga concentrations between 12
58and 25%.15,16,26,28
59There is clearly a need to elucidate the stability of
60(AlxGa1/C0 x)2O3 alloys in different structures and the evolu-
61tion of the lattice parameters. In parallel, information about
62the change in bandgap as a function of the alloy composition
63is required for device applications; a strong nonlinearity
64(“bandgap bowing”) is expected due to the structure mis-
65match between the binary endpoints. Information about band
66alignments between Ga 2O3 and Al 2O3 in their different
67phases is also currently incomplete, with experimental mea-
68surements reporting a wide spread of values. 29–32 In the

71 Our DFT calculations employ projector augmented
72 wave (PAW) potentials 33 as implemented in the Vienna Ab-
73 initio Simulation Package (VASP)34 code, with a 500 eV cut-
74 off in the plane-wave expansion and a 4 /C2 4 /C2 4 k-point grid.
75 All structures were considered converged when all residual
76 forces were smaller than 5 meV/A˚ and all stress components
77 were smaller than 50 meV/A˚ 3. Including the Ga 3 d electrons
78 in the valence is important; if they are treated as core elec-
79 trons, the corundum a phase of Ga 2O3 is 56 meV lower in
80 energy than the monoclinic b phase, which disagrees with
81 the fact that the monoclinic phase is the actual ground state
82 of Ga2O3.35 We use the common approach of adjusting the
83 mixing parameter a in the hybrid functional of Heyd,
84 Scuseria, and Ernzerhof (HSE) 36 to reproduce the experi-
85 mental bandgap. A single value of a needs to be used in all
86 calculations involving both Ga 2O3 and Al2O3. As shown in
87 Table I, a ¼ 0.32 produces good values for the well-
88 established gaps of b-Ga2O3 and a-Al2O3 and also yields
89 structural parameters, which is in very good agreement with
90 the experiment. We used 10-atom monoclinic and corundum
91 cells and explicitly considered all alloys that can be modeled
92 within these cells. Band alignments with respect to vacuum
93 were obtained from surface calculations using 120-atom
94 slabs terminated along the ð10/C2210Þ direction for corundum
95 and 100-atom slabs terminated along the (010) direction for
96 the monoclinic structure. A 20-A˚ vacuum layer, which is suf-
97 ﬁcient to eliminate interactions between periodic images,
98 was used together with a 2 /C2 4 /C2 1 k-point sampling. Full
99 relaxation of internal coordinates and in-plane lattice con-
100 stants was performed. Both surface orientations are nonpolar
101 in the sense that they contain both atom types in each layer.
102 The dependence of lattice parameters on the alloy com-
103 position is summarized in Fig. 1 by plotting the pseudocubic
104 lattice parameter (the cube root of the volume per formula
105 unit). The corundum structure has a smaller volume com-
106 pared to the monoclinic structure because it is more compact
107 due to all cations being octahedrally coordinated, while in
108 the monoclinic structure, half the sites are tetrahedrally
109coordinated. When alloying Ga 2O3 with Al, the lattice
110parameters shrink, consistent with the smaller atomic radius
111of Al compared to Ga. The change in the pseudocubic lattice
112parameter is linear, in agreement with Vegard’s law. The
113same trend is observed in the experimental values for the
114monoclinic structure, also included in Fig. 1; the fact that
115the experimental data points lie slightly above the calculated
116curve is consistent with the small underestimate of our
117calculated lattice parameters compared to the experiment
118(Table I). Our results show that the individual lattice parame-
119ters ( a, b, c, and b) closely follow Vegard’s law, and the
120values for alloys can therefore be obtained by linear interpo-
121lation of the entries in Table I.

147 Temperature effects are included in the Gibbs free
148 energy of mixing, DGðxÞ¼ DHðxÞ/C0 TDSðxÞ, where the
149 mixing entropy DSðxÞ is given by a random mixture model.
150 Full miscibility, as determined by the common tangent
151 method, is achieved at 952 K for corundum; for the mono-
152 clinic phase in the range up to x ¼ 0.5, this is achieved at
153 472 K and for x > 0.5 at 584 K. These temperatures are an
154 estimate of the upper bound since the model neglects vibra-
155 tional entropy and additional conﬁguration interactions.
156 Starting on the Ga2O3 side, the monoclinic crystal struc-
157 ture has the lowest energy; for bulk Ga 2O3, the corundum
158 phase is signiﬁcantly higher in energy (by 0.14 eV per cation)
159 than the monoclinic phase, consistent with the monoclinic
160 phase being the stable structure.35 In the alloys, the monoclinic
161 phase remains energetically preferable for Al concentrations
162 up to 71%. For a higher Al content, the corundum structure is
163 preferred, and for pure Al 2O3, the energy per cation site is
164 0.11 eV higher for monoclinic relative to the corundum ground
165 state. For x values in the vicinity of 0.71, the energy difference
166 between corundum and monoclinic is small and can easily be
167 overcome at high temperatures, explaining why some experi-
168 ments have observed the monoclinic structure for Al concen-
169 trations above 71%17–19,21,25–27 or the corundum structure for
170 Al concentrations lower than 71%.14–16,26,28
171 The energetics in the alloy strongly correlate with the pre-
172 ferred coordination environment of Al and Ga: Al atoms are
173 found to always favor octahedral positions, which is the coor-
174 dination of the cation sites in corundum. In the monoclinic
175 structure, only half the sites are octahedrally coordinated, and
176 in the alloys, we indeed ﬁnd that these sites are preferentially
177 occupied by Al atoms. This explains the relatively lowDH for
178 the Al content up to 50% Al (Fig.2). Above 50%, Al is forced
179 to incorporate on tetrahedral sites, signiﬁcantly increasingDH
180 of the monoclinic-phase alloys. The open symbols in Fig. 2
181 indicate higher-energy structures. For the monoclinic struc-
182 tures (squares), the higher energy reﬂects the energy cost for
183 Al atoms to occupy tetrahedral positions. For corundum, the
184 higher energy cost is associated with a different arrangement
185 of the cations, where having a nearest neighbor of the same
186 atom type leads to a larger energy. Details about the structure
187 and energetics of the higher-energy structures are presented in
188 the supplementary material.
189 At x ¼ 0.5, the monoclinic AlGaO 3 structure is an
190 ordered alloy with an exceptionally low enthalpy of forma-
191 tion (4 meV). In this structure, all Al atoms are on octahedral
192 sites and all Ga atoms on tetrahedral sites. This type of struc-
193 ture was previously found to be highly stable for alloys of
194 Ga2O3 with In 2O3, where In preferentially incorporates on
195 the octahedral sites. 41 Such an arrangement also minimizes
196 the local strain since the local environment around both types
197 of cations is very close to the local environment in their bulk
198 ground-state structures.

245 In conclusion, we performed a detailed ﬁrst-principles
246 study of Ga 2O3-Al2O3 alloys in both the corundum and
247 monoclinic structures. We ﬁnd that the pseudocubic lattice
248 constant decreases linearly with the increasing Al concentra-
249 tion, while the bandgap increases but exhibits signiﬁcant
250 bowing. For alloy concentrations smaller than 71% Al,
251 the monoclinic structure is the preferred structure, with Al
252 preferentially occupying the octahedral positions. This
253preference for the octahedral site also explains the unusual
254stability of the ordered monoclinic AlGaO 3 alloy. We ﬁnd
255that the technologically attractive monoclinic (Al xGa1– x)2O3
256alloys should be fully miscible over the composition range
257of 0%–50% at typical growth temperatures. Ga 2O3 and
258Al2O3 have large conduction-band offsets, 2.74 eV for mono-
259clinic and 3.24 eV for corundum structures. Band offsets in
260(AlxGa1/C0 x)2O3 alloys can be predicted by combining our cal-
261culated values for alloy bandgaps with interpolated values
262for band offsets between the binaries.
