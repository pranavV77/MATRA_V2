L10@P0: Radioactive 27Mg (t1=2 ¼ 9.5 min) was implanted into GaN of different doping types at CERN’s
L11@P0: ISOLDE facility and its lattice site determined via β− emission channeling. Following implantations
L12@P0: between room temperature and 800 °C, the majority of 27Mg occupies the substitutional Ga sites; however,
L13@P0: below 350 °C significant fractions were also found on interstitial positions ∼0.6 Å from ideal octahedral
L14@P0: sites. The interstitial fraction of Mg was correlated with the GaN doping character, being highest (up to
L15@P0: 31%) in samples doped p type with 2 × 1019 cm−3 stable Mg during epilayer growth, and lowest in
L16@P0: Si-doped n-GaN, thus giving direct evidence for the amphoteric character of Mg. Implanting above 350 °C
L17@P0: converts interstitial 27Mg to substitutional Ga sites, which allows estimating the activation energy for
L18@P0: migration of interstitial Mg as between 1.3 and 2.0 eV.

L20@P0: Mg-doped p-type GaN is nowadays a core component of
L21@P0: many optoelectronic devices which we find in our homes,
L22@P0: e.g., light emitting diodes for solid state white lighting [1] or
L23@P0: blue lasers [2]. Applications of GaN for power electronics,
L24@P0: e.g., as high voltage transistors or power converters[3,4], also
L25@P0: exist already but are less widespread; others, as in photovoltaics [5], may still emerge. Despite the technological
L26@P0: maturity of GaN, there are some basic properties related to
L27@P0: itsp-type dopingthat are still poorly understood and also limit
L28@P0: the performance of devices. One such major problem is an
L29@P0: inherent doping limit: once the Mg concentration in GaN
L30@P0: surpasses ∼1019–1020 cm−3, further introduction of Mg does
L31@P0: not lead to an increase in the hole concentration; see, e.g.,
L32@P0: Ref.[6] and references therein. The electrical activation ofMg
L33@P0: as a p-type dopant requires its incorporation on the substitutional Ga site SGa (MgGa), while Mg on other sites, e.g.,
L34@P0: interstitial Mg (Mgi), or Mg replacing N (MgN), should be
L35@P0: electrically inactive or even exhibit donor character. In the
L36@P0: earlier stages of GaN research it was suggested from ab initio
L37@P0: density functional theory calculations by Neugebauer and
L38@P0: Van de Walle [7,8] that “incorporation of the Mg atoms on the
L39@P0: N site or in an interstitial configuration were found to be
L40@P0: negligible.” This view, however, was not shared by all
L41@P0: theorists; e.g., Reboredo and Pantelides stated that “interstitial
L42@P0: Mg plays a major role in limiting p-type doping” [9,10]. The
L43@P0: failure of experimental methods in establishing the existence
L44@P0: of interstitial Mg and investigating its properties probably
L45@P0: contributed to the fact that in the following years many
L46@P0: researchers adopted the opinion that the doping limitations at
L47@P0: high Mg concentrations are, in addition to passivation by H
L48@P0: [8,11], mostly a consequence of native defects with donor
L49@P0: character, in particular N vacancies VN, either in their isolated
L50@P0: form [12,13] or in MgGa − VN complexes with Mg [14,15].
L51@P0: Recently, this controversial discussion was revived by
L52@P0: the results of hybrid density functional calculations of
L53@P0: Miceli and Pasquarello [16], who concluded that “…the
L54@P0: amphoteric nature of the Mg impurity is critical to explain
L55@P0: the dropoff in the hole density observed experimentally”:
L56@P0: once the doping limit has been reached, additional Mg
L57@P0: atoms are not incorporated on substitutional Ga sites any
L58@P0: more but on interstitial sites where they form compensating
L59@P0: double donors, thus pinning the Fermi level. The reader is
L60@P0: referred to Ref. [16] for a more detailed discussion of
L61@P0: several arguments that support this theory from the viewpoint of electrical and optical characterization methods.
L62@P0: Low formation energies for interstitial Mgi, if the Fermi
L63@P0: level is located below midgap, were also theoretically
L64@P0: predicted by Reshchikov et al. [17].
L65@P0: In contrast to the various refined but often contradictory
L66@P0: theoretical models on the structural properties of
L67@P0: Mg-related defects in GaN, no experimental data on the
L68@P0: Mg lattice sites exist so far. The often used ion beam lattice
L69@P0: location technique of Rutherford backscattering spectrometry and channeling (RBS/C) is not applicable in this
L70@P0: case since Mg is much lighter than the Ga host atoms.
L71@P0: Alternative ion beam detection methods such as particleinduced x-ray emission or nuclear reaction analysis fail at
L72@P0: the low concentrations of Mg dopants. Attempts to use
L73@P0: extended x-ray absorption fine structure for characterizing
L74@P0: the distances from Mg atoms to their nearest neighbors were
L75@P0: not successful since the Mg K x-ray absorption edge
L76@P0: overlaps with the Ga L edge [18].

L79@P0-1: 0031-9007=17=118(9)=095501(6) 095501-1 © 2017 American Physical SocietyIn this Letter, we provide direct lattice location measurements for Mg in GaN using the β− emission channeling
L80@P1: (EC) technique. We find that the majority of ion-implanted
L81@P1: Mg occupies substitutional Ga sites. However, we present
L82@P1: also direct experimental proof for the existence of interstitial Mgi on positions near the so-called octahedral sites
L83@P1: and we show that its fraction depends on the GaN doping
L84@P1: character, being enhanced in samples that were predoped p
L85@P1: type with stable Mg, and suppressed in Si-doped n-GaN.
L86@P1: We also give experimental estimates for the migration
L87@P1: energy of interstitial Mg, which are derived from its
L88@P1: microscopic interaction with Ga vacancies.
L89@P1: The EC method allows probing the sites of radioactive
L90@P1: isotopes in single-crystalline samples [19–21]. The radioactive probe atoms are implanted at low fluences and the
L91@P1: emitted β− particles are guided by the crystal potential. A
L92@P1: two-dimensional position-sensitive detector (PSD) [20,21]is
L93@P1: used to measure the angle-dependent emission yield of
L94@P1: electrons in the vicinity of major crystallographic directions,
L95@P1: providing patterns which are characteristic for the probe atom
L96@P1: lattice location in the sample. In the case of Mg, the only
L97@P1: radioactive isotope suitable for EC studies is the short-lived
L98@P1: 27Mg (t1=2 ¼ 9.45 min) which can be produced at CERN’s
L99@P1: ISOLDE on-line isotope separator facility by means of
L100@P1: bombarding Ti targets with 1.4 GeV protons, followed by
L101@P1: resonant laser ionization and mass separation [22]. This
L102@P1: isotope was recently applied with success in the emission
L103@P1: channeling study of the Mg lattice location in AlN [23].
L104@P1: The GaN layers used were grown by metal-organic
L105@P1: chemical vapor deposition at the University of Cambridge
L106@P1: and based on 4.8 μm thick wurtzite single-crystal GaN
L107@P1: epilayers on (0001) sapphire. The first type of sample simply
L108@P1: consisted of the not intentionally doped (NID) epilayer
L109@P1: (NID-GaN), while for “n-GaN:Si” samples a 150 nm thick
L110@P1: layer doped with 1 × 1019 cm−3 Si was deposited in addition. Mg predoped samples contained a 150 nm thick top
L111@P1: layer doped during growth with 2 × 1019 cm−3 Mg.
L112@P1: However, while “GaN:Mg” samples were used as grown,
L113@P1: “p-GaN:Mg” samples were annealed for 20 min at 800 °C
L114@P1: under nitrogen atmosphere in order to drive out H and
L115@P1: electrically activate the Mg [24]. While no electrical characterization was performed for the p-GaN:Mg used in
L116@P1: this experiment, typical hole concentrations and Hall mobilities for samples produced using the same equipment under
L117@P1: almost identical conditions are 1–2 × 1017 cm−3 and
L118@P1: 10–15 cm2=Vs [25].
L119@P1: The EC measurements were performed simultaneously
L120@P1: with 50 keV implantations into a 1 mm diameter beam
L121@P1: spot using the on-line setup described in Ref. [26].
L122@P1: The resulting 27Mg profiles are approximately Gaussian
L123@P1: with a typical depth of 488  244 Å. Because of its short
L124@P1: half-life of 9.45 min, there is hardly any 27Mg accumulation
L125@P1: in the samples throughout the experiments, with peak
L126@P1: concentrations remaining below 3 × 1014 cm−3
L127@P1: , while its
L128@P1: decay product 27Al is a stable isotope isoelectronic to Ga.
L129@P1: However, each implanted Mg atom initially creates several
L130@P1: hundred Ga and N vacancies, which accumulate to some
L131@P1: extent during the experiment according to the annealing
L132@P1: history of the sample. In order to derive the fractions of
L133@P1: 27Mg on different lattice sites, the experimental β− emission
L134@P1: yields are fitted by theoretical patterns corresponding to
L135@P1: 27Mg emitter atoms residing on a combination of different
L136@P1: lattice sites. Calculating the theoretical β− patterns for GaN
L137@P1: using the so-called “many-beam” formalism has been
L138@P1: outlined in Refs. [27–29], while the fit procedure is
L139@P1: described in Refs. [20,21,29]. The major lattice sites in
L140@P1: GaN are shown in Fig. 1 while typical theoretical emission
L141@P1: patterns for the major substitutional and interstitial sites are,
L142@P1: e.g., given in Refs. [23,28,29].

L299@P3: The temperature dependence for high-current implantation into the GaN:Mg sample was used to estimate the
L300@P3: migration energy (EM) of interstitial Mgi applying two
L301@P3: simple Arrhenius models. The models assume that Mgi that
L302@P3: remained interstitial following implantation starts to migrate
L303@P3: due to its thermal energy and that it requires a certain number
L304@P3: of jumps N until it encounters a Ga vacancy during the
L305@P3: lifetime of 27Mg, which will lead to the formation of MgGa
L306@P3: (see Ref. [23] for details). Besides an attempt frequency ν0,
L307@P3: for which we have taken 2 × 1013 Hz, corresponding to the
L308@P3: 657 cm−1 local mode assigned to Mg in GaN [38], N was
L309@P3: assumed ranging from 1 to 105, where N ¼ 1 represents the
L310@P3: limiting case in which theMgi has a neighboring Ga vacancy,
L311@P3: and N ¼ 105 is the upper limit when the diffusion-induced
L312@P3: widening of the Mgi profile becomes comparable to the
L313@P3: implantation depth, which can be excluded since it would
L314@P3: considerably deteriorate the channeling effects. According to
L315@P3: the least square fits shown by the solid lines in Fig. 4, the
L316@P3: migration energies are thus estimated between 2.0 and 1.3 eV,
L317@P3: respectively. Theoretical predictions for EM [39,40] have
L318@P3: suggested that the migration of Mgi in planes perpendicular
L319@P3: to the c axis is much faster than along the c axis, with EM⊥ ¼
L320@P3: 0.15 eV and EM∥ ¼ 0.68 eV, respectively. Our experimental estimates indicate a considerably higher EM value than
L321@P3: these predictions. Several conflicting results on measuring
L322@P3: the macroscopic diffusion coefficients of Mg in GaN have
L323@P3: been reported in the literature, therefore suggesting that
L324@P3: macroscopic diffusion of Mg is in fact trap limited. While
L325@P3: Chang et al.state that during 1.25 h at 1060 °C“no significant
L326@P3: diffusion/segregation effects were observed” [41], Köhler
L327@P3: et al. have determined an activation energy ED of 5 eV in the
L328@P3: temperature range 925–1050 °C [42], while Benzarti et al.
L329@P3: concluded 1.9 eV [43] and Pan and Chi 1.3 eVonly [44]. The
L330@P3: value of 1.9 eV would fit well with the microscopic migration
L331@P3: energy estimated by us. However, Benzarti et al. based
L332@P3: their analysis on only two measurements at T ¼ 1090 and
L333@P3: 1130 °C, which at such high temperatures may be a too
L334@P3: limited temperature region in order to derive trustworthy
L335@P3: parameters for D0 and ED in the exponential function.

L337@P3: interpretation of GaN doping with Mg are obvious. If Mg is
L338@P3: introduced by means of ion implantation the damage
L339@P3: caused by the implantation process has two characteristic
L340@P3: effects. First, it provides the Ga vacancies which are
L341@P3: necessary for Mg to be incorporated on substitutional Ga
L342@P3: sites. Second, accumulation of damage causes a shift of the
L343@P3: Fermi level towards midgap, which in the case of initially
L344@P3: p-type GaN also results in an increase of substitutional Mg.
